The author. Angie Thomas was born in 1988 and grew up in Jackson, Mississippi. As a teenager she was a rapper; later she studied creative writing at Belhaven University. The novel began as a short story she wrote in college, after a young Black man named Oscar Grant was shot by a police officer in 2009 (see the #BlackLivesMatter tab).

The starting point. Starr lives in Garden Heights, a poor, mostly Black neighbourhood, and goes to Williamson Prep, a private school where most students are white and wealthy. Garden Heights is fictional — but it is modelled on real American neighbourhoods like the one the author grew up in.

A title in code

The title is taken from an idea by the rapper Tupac Shakur. Read the capital letters of the first three words — they are the beginning of a longer phrase:

T
H
U
G
L
I
F
E

Tupac had these two words tattooed across his stomach. For him every letter stood for a word, and together they made a statement about society. What the letters stand for is explained in chapter 1 — so we won't give it away here.

The language of the novel

Starr tells her own story — and she sounds like a real 16-year-old, not like a textbook.

Much of the novel is written in African American Vernacular English (AAVE), the variety of English spoken in many Black communities in the USA. AAVE is not "wrong" or "lazy" English: linguists describe it as a variety with its own consistent grammar and pronunciation rules, with roots that go back centuries.

Many speakers switch between AAVE and Standard American English depending on where they are and who they are talking to. This is called code-switching — and it is one of the things to watch for from the very first chapters.

What you will notice on the page

In the novel (AAVE)

A note on offensive language. The novel contains swear words and the n-word, used by Black characters among themselves. It is part of how the characters really speak — but that does not make it a word for us to use. We will talk in class about how we deal with it when reading aloud and quoting.

#BlackLivesMatter

The novel is fiction — the names on this page are not.

The hashtag #BlackLivesMatter was first used in July 2013 by three activists — Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors and Opal Tometi — after the man who had shot the teenager Trayvon Martin was found not guilty. It grew into a worldwide movement against police violence and racism towards Black people.

Angie Thomas has said that cases like these were on her mind when she wrote the novel. Open each name to read what happened.

Why Emmett Till and Rodney King are on this list. Both cases are much older than the hashtag. They are remembered alongside the others because they show that the problem did not begin in 2012 — and because in each case a picture or a video forced the whole country to look.
Think about it
Look at how each case ended in court. What pattern do you notice — and what might that pattern do to people's trust in the justice system?

From slavery to civil rights

To understand Garden Heights, you need about 400 years of history.

1619
first enslaved Africans arrive in the English colony of Virginia
12.5M
Africans forced onto slave ships in the transatlantic slave trade (estimate)
1865
slavery abolished in the USA (13th Amendment)
~100
more years until legal segregation ended

Court action, organized protest and federal laws worked together to end legal segregation — but legal change did not end inequality. Open each entry for details.

Where is "the South"?

"The South" can mean different things. The eleven states that left the USA in 1860–61 to protect slavery and formed the Confederacy were South Carolina, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Texas, Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina.

The Deep South usually refers to South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and Louisiana — the states where the plantation economy and, later, segregation were strongest. Angie Thomas comes from Jackson, the capital of Mississippi.

Starr's world – your mind map

While you read, you build your own big map of the novel — one layer per reading section.

  1. Get started: take an A3 sheet in landscape format. Write STARR in the middle and draw the seven branches shown below.
  2. One colour per section: after each reading section, add what you have found in that section's colour. At the end you will be able to see at a glance how the story — and Starr — develops.
  3. The minimum per section: at least one new entry on every branch, and two short quotations with page numbers.
  4. Compare in class: bring your map to the first lesson after each section. Anything you add from others goes in pencil, so your own work stays visible.
  5. Keep it safe: take a photo of your map after each section. It will be the basis for your revision — and for the core sheet at the end.
