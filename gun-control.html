Guns in America

History, law and debate around the Second Amendment

Why this topic matters

Few issues reveal as much about American identity, law and political culture as the right to own a gun.

The Second Amendment to the US Constitution, ratified in 1791, protects "the right of the people to keep and bear arms." In the United States, gun ownership is treated as a constitutional right rather than a licensed privilege — a right roughly a third of American adults personally exercise. This single difference shapes everything from policing and schools to elections and popular culture.

The debate is not simply "for" or "against" guns. It is a conflict between two values that both sides claim: individual liberty and self-defence on one side, and collective safety and the right to life on the other. Courts, Congress and the public disagree sharply about where the balance should lie — and that disagreement has only sharpened in the 2020s as the Supreme Court has taken a more expansive view of Second Amendment rights.

The story of American gun law is not a straight line toward either more or less regulation. Policy shift under the second Trump administration (2025–2026)

Since February 2025, federal policy has moved to loosen rather than tighten gun rules. Executive Order 14206 ordered a review of Biden-era firearms regulations, and in April 2026 the Department of Justice rolled back 34 ATF regulations — one of the broadest rewrites of federal gun rules in the agency's history. This marks a clear reversal from the direction set by the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act (2022), even though gun-death and mass-shooting numbers have continued a multi-year decline that began independently of this policy shift.

Landmark Supreme Court cases

Six decisions define how courts read the Second Amendment today. Read top to bottom for the legal arc: from a narrow "militia" reading to a broad individual right, and now a stricter historical test that continues to be tested case by case.

1939United States v. Miller

The Court unanimously upheld the National Firearms Act, reasoning that the Second Amendment protects weapons with a reasonable relationship to militia service — not any weapon a private citizen might want. This "collective rights" reading shaped gun law for almost 70 years.

2008District of Columbia v. Heller

For the first time, the Court ruled that the Second Amendment protects an individual's right to own a firearm for self-defence in the home, independent of militia service, striking down Washington D.C.'s handgun ban. The opinion explicitly preserved bans on gun ownership by felons and the mentally ill, and restrictions in "sensitive places" such as schools.

2010McDonald v. City of Chicago

The Court extended Heller to state and local governments via the Fourteenth Amendment, meaning cities and states — not only the federal government — must respect the individual right to bear arms.

2022NY State Rifle & Pistol Assn. v. Bruen

The Court struck down New York's discretionary concealed-carry permit system and introduced a new "text, history and tradition" test: a modern gun law is only constitutional if it has a comparable historical analogue from the founding era. This raised the bar for defending existing gun laws in court.

2024United States v. Rahimi

In an 8–1 ruling, the Court upheld a federal law disarming people subject to domestic-violence restraining orders, clarifying that Bruen requires a similar historical principle, not an identical historical law.

2024Garland v. Cargill

Decided the same month as Rahimi, the Court struck down a federal ban on bump stocks — attachments that let semi-automatic rifles fire at rates close to automatic weapons — ruling that the ATF had exceeded its authority in classifying them as machine guns.

Key arguments in the debate

Both sides of the American gun debate argue from real values and real evidence. For classroom debate and essay writing, it helps to know the strongest version of each case — not a caricature of it.

Gun rights perspective

  • The Second Amendment protects an individual, fundamental right — confirmed by the Supreme Court in Heller (2008) and McDonald (2010) — that should not depend on where a citizen lives.
  • Firearms enable self-defence, especially for people in rural areas far from police, or in communities that feel under-protected.
  • Most gun owners are law-abiding; restricting them does not stop criminals, who by definition already break laws.
  • An armed citizenry is framed by many gun-rights advocates as a check on potential government overreach, in the tradition of the Founders' distrust of standing armies.
  • Gun ownership is also cultural: hunting, sport shooting and collecting are longstanding traditions in many American families and regions.
  • Stricter laws in one state are easily undermined by looser laws next door, since guns cross state lines — a point used to argue that local bans mainly burden law-abiding owners.
  • Some argue that armed staff or trained citizens can shorten the time before a threat is stopped, making total disarmament a false sense of safety.

Gun control perspective

  • The United States has a far higher rate of gun death than almost any other wealthy democracy, which gun-control advocates link directly to the sheer number of firearms in circulation.
  • Background checks, waiting periods and safe-storage rules are supported by large majorities of Americans, including many gun owners, even when broader "gun control" is not.
  • Mass shootings at schools (Sandy Hook, Parkland, Uvalde) and other public places have made gun violence a leading cause of death for American children and teenagers.
  • The "text, history and tradition" test set out in Bruen (2022) is criticised as an unworkable standard that ties 21st-century safety policy to 18th-century circumstances.
  • Comparisons with countries such as the UK, Australia and Germany, which tightened gun laws after mass shootings and saw firearm deaths fall, are cited as evidence that regulation works.
  • Easy access to firearms is strongly linked to a higher risk of suicide, since guns make impulsive attempts far more likely to be fatal than other methods.
  • Advocates argue that the gun lobby's political influence, not public opinion, is what blocks popular measures such as universal background checks from becoming federal law.

Public opinion sits between these poles and shifts with events: a June 2023 Pew Research Center survey found 58% of US adults wanted stricter gun laws, 26% thought current laws were about right, and 15% wanted them loosened — but the same research shows deep partisan division, with roughly 80–90% of Democrats and only around a quarter to a third of Republicans favouring stricter laws.

Spotlight: the NRA

Founded in 1871 as a marksmanship organisation, the National Rifle Association (NRA) became the country's most influential gun-rights lobbying group from the 1970s onward, funding political campaigns and helping block measures such as the renewal of the 1994 assault weapons ban — a key reason gun-control advocates cite the gun lobby's influence, not public opinion, as the main obstacle to reform. Its influence has weakened recently: a New York fraud trial found long-time leader Wayne LaPierre liable for misusing millions of dollars in NRA funds, he resigned in 2024, and membership-dues revenue fell from about $83 million in 2022 to $51.7 million in 2024. Even so, the NRA endorsed Donald Trump in 2024, and gun-rights groups remain a powerful voice in shaping federal policy.
