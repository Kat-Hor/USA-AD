The foreign-born population reached an all-time record of 53.3 million (15.8%) in January 2025. By June 2025 it had fallen to 51.9 million (15.4%) — the first decline in the US immigrant population since the 1960s — yet this is still a higher share than at any point in US history except the single year of 1890.

California is home to the most immigrants overall (10+ million, ~27% of the state's population), followed by Texas, Florida and New York.

Legal immigration pathways: the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) allows up to 675,000 permanent immigrant visas per year across all categories — plus an unlimited number for immediate relatives (spouses, parents, minor children) of US citizens. Family-based immigration remains the largest single pathway into the US, ahead of employment-based visas, refugee/asylum admissions, and the Diversity Visa Lottery.

Pull Factors: Then & Now

"Pull factors" are the reasons that attract people to a country, as opposed to "push factors," which drive people away from their home country. Many of the pull factors that brought immigrants to the US through Ellis Island in the 19th and early 20th century are still present today — but the picture has also changed considerably.

Then (c. 1880–1920)
Free or cheap farmland available through policies like the Homestead Act, attracting settlers to the Midwest and West.
Now
High-skilled and service-sector jobs in a large, developed economy — from Silicon Valley tech jobs to agricultural and construction work.
Then
Rapid industrialisation created a huge demand for factory labour in growing cities like New York and Chicago.
Now
A persistent demand for labour in specific sectors (agriculture, construction, healthcare, hospitality) that is hard to fill domestically.
Then
Religious and political freedom — escaping persecution (e.g. Jewish immigrants fleeing pogroms in the Russian Empire, the group Emma Lazarus advocated for).
Now
Asylum and refugee protection for people fleeing war, political persecution, or gang violence, particularly from Central America, Venezuela, and other crisis regions.
Then
Family or community members already settled in the US, writing home and encouraging others to follow ("chain migration").
Now
Family reunification remains one of the main legal pathways into the US — most green cards are still issued on this basis.
Then
A relatively open immigration policy for European arrivals before the restrictive quota laws of the 1920s.
Now
A globally recognised university and research system that draws international students, many of whom stay through work visas after graduating.
Then
The powerful cultural image of America as a "land of opportunity," reinforced by literature, posters and word of mouth.
Now
The same cultural image persists globally through media, social networks and diaspora communities — even as US immigration policy has become far more restrictive and enforcement-heavy.
Discussion point: Which of these pull factors do you think has changed the most since the 19th century — and which has stayed almost exactly the same?

Why these two belong together

ICE and Dreamers are covered as a single topic because they are directly linked: ICE decides whether and when to enforce a removal, and a Dreamer's DACA status only delays that decision — it never fully guarantees protection from it. Dreamers & DACA "Dreamers" is the informal name for people brought to the US as undocumented children who grew up there, often with no memory of another country. Since 2012, eligible Dreamers can apply for DACA (Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals) — it grants neither citizenship nor a green card, but temporarily protects against deportation and allows a renewable work permit.

DACA's legal status: still unresolved

ICE and the scale of enforcement

  • ICE officers reportedly work under a daily arrest quota (widely reported as around 3,000 arrests per day).
  • ICE detention reached a record 73,400 people on a single day in mid-January 2026.
  • Long-standing guidance against ICE operations in "sensitive locations" such as schools, hospitals and places of worship has been ended.
  • Local and state police can now be deputised to act as immigration agents under expanded "287(g)" agreements.
  • The Laken Riley Act expanded mandatory, no-bond detention to people merely arrested — not convicted — of certain crimes.

The debate

Arguments made by supporters

  • Consistent enforcement of immigration law, supporters argue, is necessary for the rule of law to mean anything.
  • Removing "sensitive location" protections and quotas is defended as ensuring the law applies equally everywhere, not just in some places.
  • Some supporters argue that even Dreamers, though raised in the US, entered unlawfully as a matter of fact and that only Congress — not executive action — should be able to grant permanent status.

Arguments made by critics

  • Critics argue that deporting Dreamers who have lived in the US since childhood, often with no memory of another country, is disproportionate and inhumane.
  • Arrests at schools, hospitals and courthouses are criticised for discouraging people — including US citizens in mixed-status families — from seeking essential services out of fear.
  • Civil liberties groups warn that arrest quotas create pressure to detain people without sufficient evidence or due process.
  • Critics point to the economic and social cost of deporting long-settled workers, students and parents of US-citizen children.
DACA's legal status can change quickly through new court rulings. As of September 2026, current recipients can still renew — but the underlying program remains legally unresolved and no group has certainty about the future.
